# Rocket Lab Buys Iridium for $8B. Peter Beck Is Building the Anti-SpaceX.

**Date:** 2026-06-29

Rocket Lab announced this morning it will acquire Iridium Communications in an $8 billion cash-and-stock deal — [a 24% premium](https://spacenews.com/rocket-lab-to-acquire-iridium/) over Friday's close, with Iridium shareholders getting $27 in cash plus Rocket Lab shares equaling $54 per share total. The deal expected to close mid-2027.

The headline reads "Rocket Lab takes on SpaceX." That framing is lazy. What Peter Beck is actually doing is more interesting and more deliberate than a rivalry.

## The Spectrum Shortcut

Beck's been telegraphing a move into satellite services for over a year, telling analysts the company planned its own constellation. But he never said what kind, and — crucially — Rocket Lab never filed for spectrum, which is the foundational step to actually operating satellite communications.

That's because Beck was shopping.

Here's the thing about L-band spectrum — the radio frequencies Iridium uses: **you cannot make more of it.** It's globally harmonized, internationally licensed, and finite. Iridium has held this spectrum for decades. It's the reason Iridium can deliver reliable connectivity in the middle of the Arctic Ocean, on a container ship, or in a cockpit over the Pacific where no terrestrial network exists.

Building a new constellation from scratch to do what Iridium does would take 10+ years and billions in capital before you earn your first dollar of services revenue. Beck called this acquisition "a shortcut." That's one of the more honest things an executive has said about a deal in a while. He's right.

## What Rocket Lab Actually Just Became

Before this deal, Rocket Lab was two businesses: launch (Electron rockets, with Neutron in development) and space systems (building satellites and components for others). Launch is the part everyone knows. But look at the revenue mix: analysts expected Rocket Lab's space systems division to generate $162 million in Q2 revenue against $71 million from launch. The satellite hardware business is already bigger.

Now add Iridium's recurring revenue base. [2.55 million active subscribers](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/29/rocket-lab-buys-iridium.html) across government, defense, aviation, maritime, and commercial markets — customers who pay monthly for connectivity that works when nothing else does. Iridium's revenue is relatively predictable and its customer base is sticky. Airlines and navies don't churn.

So after the deal closes, Rocket Lab is:
- A launch provider (Electron now, Neutron coming)
- A satellite manufacturer (Photon bus, components for third parties)
- A satellite services operator (Iridium's L-band network)
- A PNT services company (Iridium's GPS-augmentation push)
- An aviation data services company (Aireon, [which Iridium acquired in May](https://spacenews.com/iridium-to-take-over-aireon-in-367-million-deal-to-expand-aviation-safety-business/) for $367M)

That's vertical integration in a way no other company except SpaceX has achieved in the space industry.

## The SpaceX Comparison Is Real, But Overstated

Yes, SpaceX built the same playbook: rockets → satellites → services revenue (Starlink). And yes, Rocket Lab is explicitly following it. But there's a meaningful difference in market focus.

Starlink is chasing consumer broadband and direct-to-device for smartphones. It's a mass market product. It needs to be cheap and ubiquitous.

Iridium's L-band network serves a different customer entirely — one who needs *reliability over bandwidth*, often in contested, remote, or safety-critical environments. A Starlink connection is better than Iridium for watching Netflix on a ship. An Iridium connection is better than Starlink for a pilot who needs a weather uplink over the Atlantic during a solar storm when Ku-band links are degraded.

Beck knows this. Iridium has spent years positioning its NTN Direct service as *complementary* to Starlink, not competitive. That framing remains accurate post-acquisition.

What Rocket Lab gains isn't a Starlink killer. It's a defense and government anchor business with 2.5 million subscribers and globally harmonized spectrum that nobody can replicate from scratch.

## Why The $3.6 Billion Bridge Loan Is The Story Nobody Is Telling

Rocket Lab's market cap before this deal was around $49 billion. [Iridium is valued at $8 billion](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/rocket-lab-to-acquire-iridium-in-historic-deal-creating-a-fully-vertically-integrated-space-powerhouse-primed-for-growth-302813075.html). To fund the $27/share cash component, Rocket Lab has committed bridge financing of $3.6 billion from Deutsche Bank and Wells Fargo — a 364-day facility. That's a short fuse.

This means Rocket Lab will need to refinance into permanent debt or raise equity capital within a year of closing (expected mid-2027). The company is betting that by then, the combination story will be compelling enough to attract investors at favorable rates. That's not an unreasonable bet — but it is a bet. If capital markets tighten or the integration looks messy, that bridge loan becomes expensive.

This is also why Rocket Lab's stock jumped 9% today while Iridium's soared 20%: investors think Rocket Lab got a bargain and is paying a fair but not crazy premium. The market has priced Iridium's strategic value into Rocket Lab's share count.

## The Globalstar Parallel

It's worth remembering what the comparable transaction looked like: Amazon acquired Globalstar — Iridium's direct competitor in mobile satellite services — for $11 billion in April. Amazon got Globalstar's Ka-band and S-band spectrum for its direct-to-device ambitions (primarily for the iPhone emergency SOS use case and its own device ecosystem).

That deal sent every satellite analyst looking for the next domino. Iridium was the obvious one. The question was only who would buy it.

Beck moved before anyone else. The fact that this deal is happening within three months of the Amazon-Globalstar announcement suggests Rocket Lab was already in conversations or moved extremely quickly once Globalstar was gone.

## What This Means for the Space Industry

The mid-2020s space economy story is consolidating from "lots of startups launching things" to "vertically integrated operators with launch, manufacturing, and services under one roof." SpaceX pioneered it. Amazon is building toward it through Project Kuiper plus Globalstar. Now Rocket Lab joins that tier.

Left out: everyone else. Legacy satellite operators without launch capabilities. Launch providers without services revenue. Pure-play satellite manufacturers who build for others.

The commercial space industry is becoming like the cloud industry circa 2015: a few large integrated players with full-stack offerings, plus a long tail of specialists who build on top of or alongside them. Iridium's 66-satellite constellation wasn't going to get there alone. Under Rocket Lab, it becomes the communications layer of a fully vertical space company — and a serious government anchor for whatever constellation Rocket Lab builds next.

Peter Beck called it "Rocket Lab magic." The honest version of that is: patient capital, smart acquisitions, and a willingness to buy what would take a decade to build.
