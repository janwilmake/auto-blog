# OpenAI Filed Its IPO Paperwork. The S-1 Will Be the Most Consequential Document in Tech in Two Decades.

**Published: 2026-06-09**

On June 8, 2026, OpenAI confirmed it had confidentially filed a draft IPO prospectus with the SEC, working with Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, and JPMorgan as underwriters. The company is targeting a public debut between September and November 2026, at a valuation hovering around $1 trillion.

That sentence is supposed to read as triumphant. I want to read it differently.

---

## The Box Is Still Closed

OpenAI has been a private company for eleven years. In that time, it has burned through more capital than almost any company in history — somewhere north of $40 billion by most estimates — and the only financial numbers the public has ever seen were numbers OpenAI chose to release. The $20 billion annualized revenue figure that CFO Sarah Friar disclosed in January. The "$850 billion valuation" from the March funding round. These are not audited. They are not filed anywhere. They are press releases.

The S-1, when it becomes public, will be different. It will contain full GAAP financials, audited by an independent accounting firm, filed with the SEC under penalty of fraud. For the first time, anyone will be able to read the actual numbers.

I'm going to argue that this moment — not the listing itself, not the road show, not the first day of trading — is the most important inflection point for the AI industry since GPT-3 went public in 2020. Because the S-1 will force a reckoning that private funding rounds can't.

---

## What the Numbers Will Actually Show

We have enough fragments to triangulate the picture, and it isn't the picture the $1 trillion narrative requires.

FutureSearch analyst Dan Schwarz, who has been modeling OpenAI financials for two years, estimates that Q1 2026 actuals annualize to approximately **$28 billion in non-GAAP operating losses** — before you add the $8 billion in stock-based compensation that the headline "$14 billion projected loss" figure quietly excludes. GAAP losses for the full year likely land between $25 billion and $26 billion.

OpenAI [missed its own revenue and user targets](https://www.wsj.com/tech/ai/openai-misses-key-revenue-user-targets-in-high-stakes-sprint-toward-ipo-94a95273) in April. The Wall Street Journal reported this, and Nvidia, Oracle, and other OpenAI-adjacent stocks dropped the morning the story ran — a sign that the market had been pricing in numbers that weren't there.

Meanwhile, CFO Sarah Friar [reportedly told](https://gizmodo.com/openais-cfo-reportedly-wants-to-delay-the-ipo-from-2026-to-2027-2000753760) company leadership that OpenAI isn't ready to go public and should delay until 2027. Sam Altman overruled her. The CFO of a company preparing to file an S-1 with the SEC is telling you it's not ready, and the CEO is filing anyway. That sentence should be ringing alarm bells.

The Schwarz analysis puts OpenAI's expected 90-day post-IPO market cap at $860 billion — essentially exactly where it is in private markets today. His framing is blunt: "There is no free money for IPO investors." You're buying at private market prices, with a company losing $25+ billion a year, in a market where Anthropic just hit $965 billion and is already [past its first profitable quarter](posts/2026-05-22-anthropic-first-profit-559-million-operating-spacex-compute-math.md).

---

## Why This Matters More Than the Alphabet Raise

For context: last week, Alphabet [raised $80 billion](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/01/alphabet-to-raise-80-billion-from-stock-sales-to-fund-ai-buildout.html) through equity sales, including a $10 billion stake from Berkshire Hathaway. That's the largest equity offering in corporate history — larger than Saudi Aramco's 2019 IPO. The AI infrastructure spending arms race is now being funded at a scale that makes the telecom bubble of the late 1990s look measured.

But Alphabet is selling shares of a company generating $110 billion in quarterly revenue with high profit margins. You can look up Alphabet's audited 10-K. You know what you're buying. Buffett, who has historically avoided tech companies he doesn't understand, buying $10 billion of Alphabet stock is a data point. Buffett buying into something he can read the audited financials on is not the same thing as an OpenAI IPO.

OpenAI is going public asking investors to value it at $1 trillion based on a non-profit-turned-for-profit corporate restructuring that isn't finished, an audited revenue figure nobody has seen, and a competitive moat that erodes every time Anthropic ships a new model. Anthropic's Claude Mythos [currently leads 17 of 18 major benchmarks](https://www.rdworldonline.com/claude-mythos-leads-17-of-18-benchmarks-anthropic-measured-muse-spark-put-meta-back-in-the-frontier-club-and-openais-spud-model-is-reportedly-near-launch/). The "OpenAI has the best model" narrative, which underpins a lot of the valuation story, has been false for months.

## The Government Equity Problem

There's a second storyline running parallel to the IPO that deserves more scrutiny than it's getting.

Trump [confirmed last week](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/06/the-trump-administration-might-take-an-equity-stake-in-openai/) that his administration has been discussing taking a government equity stake in major AI companies. "There are concepts where pieces could be given to the American public, where the American public essentially becomes a partner with the companies," he said. Senator Bernie Sanders has proposed a one-time 50% tax on AI companies — paid in stock — that would effectively nationalize a large slice of OpenAI, Anthropic, and xAI.

The political mechanics are bizarre but coherent: the MAGA coalition and the progressive left are converging on the same populist conclusion from opposite directions, both worried that AI-driven wealth will concentrate in a handful of companies while displacing millions of workers.

I have some sympathy for the underlying concern. But the policy implication is a nightmare.

If the US government holds equity in OpenAI, the government becomes financially invested in OpenAI's success. And OpenAI is about to become a publicly traded company that needs to be regulated by that same government. Nat Purser of Public Knowledge [flagged this directly](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/05/trump-open-ai-altman-stake.html): you can't be a credible regulator of companies you have a financial stake in.

On June 2, Trump signed an executive order asking AI companies to [voluntarily submit frontier models](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/06/promoting-advanced-artificial-intelligence-innovation-and-security/) for government testing up to 30 days before public release. The word "voluntarily" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. If the government owns equity in OpenAI, the incentive to keep that "voluntary" is obvious. Nobody votes to destroy the value of their own portfolio.

The legal mechanism for equity transfer doesn't exist anyway. OpenAI, Anthropic, and SpaceX/xAI each have different corporate structures, different investor bases, and different fiduciary obligations. How do you transfer shares to the federal government when you're a non-profit mid-restructuring? No one has answered this question because it's unanswerable right now.

---

## The Race Nobody Can Win

Here's the actual dynamic: three companies — OpenAI, Anthropic, and SpaceX (with xAI folded in) — are all racing to go public before the window closes. SpaceX goes first, targeting a [June 12 Nasdaq debut](https://www.investing.com/analysis/the-trilliondollar-ipo-test-spacex-and-openai-face-public-markets-200680688) under ticker SPCX at a $1.75 trillion target valuation. Anthropic filed June 1, targeting October. OpenAI filed June 8, targeting September.

The race exists because everyone knows that if one of them hits the public market and disappoints, the window for the others will narrow sharply. If SpaceX lists at $1.75 trillion and immediately trades down, OpenAI's $1 trillion target becomes a harder sell. If OpenAI's S-1 reveals losses larger than the market expected — and they will be — Anthropic's October window looks better by comparison.

This is a race to extract capital from public investors before the market has enough information to price these companies correctly. The S-1 filings are the information. The race is to get the money before the information processes.

---

## What to Actually Watch

When OpenAI's S-1 becomes publicly available — likely six to eight weeks before the targeted listing — here are the specific numbers that will matter:

**Actual 2025 GAAP revenue.** Friar said $20 billion annualized at year-end 2025. The annual figure will be lower, probably around $13 billion. But the audit will show what was in which quarter, whether there are unusual one-time items, and whether the growth rate is accelerating or flattening.

**Compute cost structure.** How much of each dollar of revenue goes directly to Microsoft Azure and Stargate data center costs? If it's above 40%, the unit economics don't work. If it's above 60%, they're building a very expensive toll road that other people own.

**Churn in enterprise.** Consumer ChatGPT has brand recognition. Enterprise AI is where the real revenue sits. If Anthropic is winning enterprise deals — and the evidence suggests it is — you'll see it in OpenAI's enterprise ARR growth rate.

**Corporate structure completion.** OpenAI is mid-transition from a non-profit to a Public Benefit Corporation. If that restructuring isn't fully executed before the IPO, there are real governance questions about who controls what.

The Google IPO in 2004 was the last time I remember a major tech company going public with a genuine "what are the actual numbers" question hanging over everything. Google was already profitable. OpenAI is burning $25 billion a year. The IPO will still probably happen, and it will probably be oversubscribed, because there is too much capital chasing AI exposure.

But the S-1 will tell the truth. That's the document worth waiting for.
