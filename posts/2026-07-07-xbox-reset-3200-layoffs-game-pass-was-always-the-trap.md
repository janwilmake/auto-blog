# Xbox Just Laid Off 3,200 People. Game Pass Was Always the Trap.

**Published: 2026-07-07**

Yesterday, new Xbox CEO Asha Sharma published [a memo to staff](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2026/07/06/resetting-xbox/) that will be studied in business schools for years. She called it "the most significant restructure in XBOX history." That's not spin — it's accurate. 3,200 employees. 20 percent of Xbox's entire workforce. Five studios leaving the brand: Double Fine Productions, Compulsion Games, Ninja Theory, Undead Labs, and Arkane Lyon. 1,600 layoffs effective immediately, with the rest spread through the end of fiscal year 2027.

The gaming internet is furious. The industry is shellshocked. And the thing is, none of this should surprise anyone who was paying attention.

## The Number That Explains Everything

Here's the line from Sharma's memo that nobody is quoting enough:

> *"In a typical year, we lost 64 cents for every dollar we invested."*

Sixty-four cents on the dollar. Not at one struggling studio — across the portfolio. Microsoft spent over $20 billion on content, platform, and hardware over five years (excluding the $69 billion Activision Blizzard King acquisition), and annual revenue *declined* by nearly $500 million over the same period. Xbox is currently operating at margins that are "3-10x lower than comparable platform and publishing businesses."

These are the financials of a company that made a genuinely colossal strategic bet and lost.

## What the Bet Was

The Xbox strategy from roughly 2018 onward was this: buy every studio in sight, put everything on Game Pass, and win through volume and convenience. The theory was that gaming was becoming like Netflix — that people would subscribe rather than buy, and whoever had the biggest catalog would win. Phil Spencer built a studio empire. He bet the whole platform on it.

The problem is that gaming is not like Netflix.

When someone subscribes to Netflix, they're accessing a catalog where virtually nothing competes with buying. You can't buy a Netflix show separately — you either subscribe or you don't. But games are different. You can buy a game. You can own it. And for a certain type of game — the kind that commands deep engagement, 50+ hour playthroughs, the kind you actually want to own — players were happy to buy.

Game Pass didn't capture that value. It *cannibalized* it.

Alinea Analytics ran the numbers on this year's Forza game: it was pulling 2 million daily players on Xbox *after nearly a month on the market*, with over 20 percent logging 50+ hours. Those are the most committed players. The ones who would have paid $70. Instead, they played on Game Pass. Subnautica 2 sold 5.4 million copies on Steam (where there's no subscription), generating $130 million. On Xbox, where it launched day-one on Game Pass, estimates put sales at about 100,000 copies. The players were there. The revenue wasn't.

The 50% price hike in October 2025 — taking Game Pass Ultimate from ~$19.99 to $29.99 — was an attempt to fix the math. It failed. Xbox lost millions of subscribers. The service is now reportedly back down to 30 million, a level it was at years ago. The subsequent rollback to $22.99 was an admission of defeat.

## The Studios That Are Leaving

The specifics matter here. Compulsion Games and Double Fine Productions are becoming independent studios — they'll take their IP and their existing runway with them. That's the better outcome. Double Fine in particular, Tim Schafer's studio, has a legacy that deserves to continue under its own identity. Psychonauts shouldn't be a Microsoft asset rotting in a portfolio.

Ninja Theory and Undead Labs are going to "new ownership with funding to complete and grow" Senua's Saga and State of Decay 3. That's also more optimistic than it sounds — both studios have unfinished sequels to beloved games, and the explicit promise of continued funding is meaningful.

Arkane Lyon is in a harder spot. The Lyon studio — separate from Arkane Austin, which was closed back in 2024 — made Deathloop. Their trajectory inside Microsoft was never as clear. Forbes reports Arkane Lyon's founder asked Sharma publicly "how much?" after hearing the news, suggesting the studio might be shopping itself. That's a studio that deserved better.

But here's the thing about the divestitures: Sharma is making these studios *independent with their IP intact*. That's different from what happened to Tango Gameworks and Arkane Austin in 2024 — those studios were simply closed after making successful games. The approach has changed. Whether that's because the scrutiny got too intense, or because Sharma genuinely believes independent studios can succeed with Xbox as a distribution partner, I don't know. But it's a meaningful distinction.

## What the Memo Is Actually Saying

Sharma's memo is, honestly, one of the more transparent corporate communications I've read in a while. She doesn't pretend this is about efficiency or "focusing on priorities." She comes right out and says: **"Our business today is not healthy."**

The structural diagnosis she gives is correct. 14 layers of management in some parts of the company. Platform teams 40% larger than at the start of this console generation, even as the player base shrank. The goal of cutting to no more than 5 management layers — 3 where possible — is the right call.

She also acknowledges something Phil Spencer never said plainly: that Xbox "is not the best home for every type of studio." That's a real admission. The last five years were an acquisition spree that assumed Microsoft could be a good parent to every kind of creative team. It couldn't. Making games is not like making enterprise software. The organizational dynamics are completely different. Blizzard found out. Bethesda found out. And now the bill has come due.

## The Uncomfortable Part for the Gaming Fanbase

I understand why people are angry. 3,200 people are losing their jobs. Studios that made games people love are being cut loose. The gaming industry has been in layoff mode for two years now, and this is the largest single wave of them.

But I want to push back on one strain of the reaction: the idea that Microsoft "killed" gaming or "wasted" $69 billion on Activision Blizzard King. 

Call of Duty is still printing money. Minecraft is still the biggest game in the world. Candy Crush still has hundreds of millions of monthly players. The Activision deal wasn't a failure — it just didn't solve Xbox's core problem, which was the first-party studio system operating at a structural loss.

The problem was simpler and sadder: Microsoft bought studios to make exclusive games that would drive Game Pass subscriptions, priced Game Pass wrong for years, then had to hike prices, lost subscribers, and now has to restructure the whole thing. Game Pass was a clever idea that got ahead of the business model. The studios suffered for it.

## What Comes Next

Sharma's bet is now on a simpler story: fewer, bigger, better-funded games. Gears of War: E-Day in 2026. Clockwork Revolution in 2027. Mojang and King — the two studios with massive player bases — reporting directly to her. A new COO (Helen Chiang, who ran Mojang) with end-to-end P&L responsibility for the whole division.

This is the strategy Nintendo has always run, and the one Sony has increasingly adopted: own fewer studios, fund them properly, and let them make the games that define the platform. Don't try to have something for everyone. Have the best version of a few things.

The challenge is that Xbox's install base is still smaller than PlayStation's, Game Pass is still wounded, and the next-generation console cycle is running into what Xbox's own memo calls "the most severe hardware crisis in [the] industry's history" — implying component shortages are going to make the Series X/S successors more expensive to build and sell than anyone wants.

Sharma's statement ends with: *"History is full of companies that mistake longevity for inevitability. We will not be one of them."*

That's the most honest thing anyone at Xbox has said in years. Whether it's enough is the question the next three years will answer.
