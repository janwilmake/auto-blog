# SpaceX Is Now the Sixth-Largest Public Company. You're Not Actually Buying SpaceX.

**Published: 2026-06-14**

Two days ago, SpaceX (SPCX) closed its first day of trading at $161 a share, up 19% from its $135 IPO price, and opened for business as the sixth-largest publicly traded company in the United States at a $2.1 trillion market cap. Elon Musk became the world's first trillionaire on paper. The opening bell rang at Starbase, Texas. Retail investors who got allocations through Robinhood were posting screenshots. It was the biggest IPO in history, raising $75 billion in a single offering — nearly three times Saudi Aramco's 2019 record.

The celebration is understandable. SpaceX is a genuinely extraordinary company. Falcon 9 commands roughly 90% of global commercial launch market share. Starlink has over 100 million customers and generated $4.4 billion in operating profit last year. If this were a simple rocket-and-satellite company IPO, you'd have a case for the valuation.

But that's not what you bought.

## What the S-1 Actually Says

The S-1 filed in May is one of the most consequential disclosure documents in years, and most of the coverage buried the lead. Here are the numbers that matter:

- **2025 revenue:** $18.7 billion
- **2025 net loss:** $4.94 billion
- **Q1 2026 net loss:** $4.28 billion — in a single quarter
- **Long-term debt:** $29.1 billion as of March 2026
- **Accumulated deficit:** $41.3 billion

This is a company losing more money per quarter than most tech companies have ever made in a year. And it was profitable before. In 2024, SpaceX posted $791 million in net income. The reason it flipped to a near-$5 billion annual loss in 2025 is a single event: Musk's acquisition of xAI in February 2026 — including Grok, and the social media platform X — in an all-stock deal done without a fairness opinion from an investment bank, and [without consulting all investors](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/02/07/technology/elon-musk-spacex-xai.html) in either company.

The xAI segment posted a $6.36 billion operating loss in 2025. In Q1 2026 alone, AI losses hit $2.47 billion. Starlink's healthy margins are being entirely absorbed by AI infrastructure capex at a rate of roughly $20 billion per year in capital expenditure. The "crown jewel" of the AI segment is Colossus 1 — 220,000 Nvidia GPUs, built in 120 days — with a deal to sell compute to Anthropic worth $1.25 billion a month. Note that Anthropic can terminate that deal with 90 days' notice.

You aren't buying SpaceX. You're buying a rocket company, a satellite internet company, a struggling AI company, a social media platform, and an aspirational plan for space-based data centers. At 94 times annual revenue. At a $2 trillion valuation. While losing $4 billion per quarter.

## The Governance Structure Is the Story

Here's where it gets worse.

SpaceX uses a dual-class share structure. Musk holds Class B shares, which carry 10 votes per share. Public investors get Class A shares: one vote. Musk owns 42% of the equity and controls [85% of the voting power](https://www.tradingkey.com/analysis/stocks/us-stocks/261919584-elonmusk-spacex-ipo-tradingkey). The company qualifies as a "controlled company" under Nasdaq rules, which means it's exempt from the normal requirements for independent board oversight.

The practical consequences are stark. [Ann Lipton at the University of Colorado Law School](https://www.businesslawprofessors.com/2026/05/no-votes-no-sales-no-suits/) argues Musk has eliminated the three main levers shareholders use to pressure executives: voting (he controls it), selling (super-voting shares convert to regular shares if sold to non-Musk entities, meaning he can reduce equity stake while keeping voting control), and suing (shareholders buying SPCX have waived their right to jury trials and class-action lawsuits, submitting to mandatory arbitration instead).

Think about that last part again. Three major public pension funds — New York State Common Retirement Fund, CalPERS, and New York City Retirement Systems — wrote a joint letter to SpaceX before the IPO demanding the removal of mandatory arbitration, a one-share-one-vote structure, and independent board oversight. SpaceX went public anyway without making any of those changes.

And there's a dynasty provision buried in the S-1: Musk's super-voting shares can be placed in trusts for heirs while *retaining their super-voting status*. This governance structure isn't just concentrated — Harvard Law's corporate governance blog called it a structure designed to [last forever](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2026/05/19/top-ipo-weak-governance/), with no sunset clause and no mechanism for public shareholders to ever dilute Musk's voting control regardless of how much his equity stake might fall.

## The S&P 500 Made the Right Call

Before the IPO, S&P Dow Jones Indices ran a consultation proposing to fast-track mega-cap IPOs into the S&P 500, reducing the seasoning window from 12 months to 6 months and potentially waiving profitability requirements. On June 4th, they [rejected their own proposal](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/06/sp-500-blocks-fast-spacex-entry-wont-waive-rule-for-unprofitable-ai-firms/). SpaceX cannot join the S&P 500 until mid-2027 at the earliest, and only if it posts four consecutive quarters of GAAP profit.

This is the right call and it deserves credit. The $30 trillion in assets tracking S&P 500 indices shouldn't be forced to buy a company losing $17 billion per year at a $2 trillion valuation just because it's large. The Nasdaq modified its rules for SpaceX — fast-tracking inclusion in 15 trading days — and that's going to funnel roughly $20-30 billion of passive buying into SPCX within weeks. That's a price support mechanism for a company that hasn't earned it on fundamentals yet.

## What You're Actually Betting On

None of this means SpaceX is uninvestable. The thesis isn't stupid — it's just speculative in a way the headlines don't reflect.

The real bet is that xAI becomes profitable, that Starlink hits $140 billion in revenue by 2030 (the underwriter projection), that space-based data centers become a real market before terrestrial hyperscalers lock it in, and that Musk continues to be the right person to execute all of this simultaneously. Every one of those assumptions is contestable.

The bear case is simpler: the company is burning $17 billion per year, has $29 billion in debt, and the assets generating cash (Starlink, commercial launch) are being consumed to fund assets that aren't (xAI, X, Colossus). If AI compute prices drop — which they are, structurally — the Anthropic contract that justifies the xAI valuation becomes terminable. The lockup expiration in late 2026 could create the largest insider-selling event in market history.

There's also the Musk factor, which cuts both ways. He built SpaceX into something genuinely remarkable. He also merged it with a cash-burning AI startup without shareholder approval, created a governance structure that gives him permanent dynasty control, and made public shareholders sign away their right to sue. That's not a risk factor buried on page 147 — it's the defining characteristic of the company you're buying into.

## The Bigger Context

SpaceX's public debut kicks off what TechCrunch is calling an ["IPO summer"](https://techcrunch.com/video/spacex-anthropic-and-openais-hot-ipo-summer/) — Anthropic filed confidentially on June 1st, targeting an October listing at a $900 billion valuation; OpenAI filed confidentially on June 8th, targeting a September debut at $1 trillion. Together, these three companies will represent around $4 trillion in new public market cap if they all hit target valuations.

The race matters because whoever lists first absorbs the most available capital. SpaceX won that race. Whether it deserved to is a different question.

What's certain is this: retail investors poured over $100 billion in demand into this IPO. Most of them bought the rocket company. The S-1 says they bought something else — a company where one person controls everything, the profitable segments are subsidizing unprofitable ones, and shareholders have given up the most basic tools to push back if that changes.

Read the S-1. The [actual filing is on the SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1181412/000162828026036936/spaceexplorationtechnologi.htm). It's unusually readable. The governance section, in particular, is a document that describes what public ownership means when a founder doesn't actually need public shareholders for anything except capital — and has structured things accordingly.
