# 60% of Consumers Find "AI" in Brand Messaging a Turnoff. Nobody in Marketing Believes It.

**June 20, 2026**

WordPress VIP released a [survey this week](https://wpvip.com/future-of-the-web-2026/) of 2,000 US consumers and enterprise decision-makers that contains one of the most fascinating contradictions in recent tech marketing data.

The headline finding: **60% of US consumers say that when a brand uses the word "AI" in its messaging, it's a turnoff, not a feature.**

The contradictory finding, buried three sections later: **60% of enterprise decision-makers say traffic from AI search engines increased for their properties over the past year.** And 74% of enterprise teams say AI discoverability is now a top priority.

So enterprise marketing teams are racing to optimize for "AI" while their actual customers are being repelled by it. This is not a small misalignment. It is a catastrophic one.

---

## The Numbers Are Worse Than the Headline

The 60% turnoff figure is striking. But the fuller picture is bleaker:

- **61% of consumers couldn't name a single brand they think uses AI well in its messaging.** Not vaguely. Not "I can't think of one right now." Couldn't name one at all.
- **16% said they don't believe any brand uses AI well.** Full category rejection.
- Only 1 in 4 respondents could name a company at all when asked to name a brand doing AI messaging well.
- **42% of consumers trust AI-generated answers without attribution less than airline fees, confusing privacy policies, and medical bills.** Let that specific comparison sink in. The standard for untrustworthy information in American life used to be fine print and junk mail. Now it's "like AI-generated content."
- **74% say the internet feels less human than it did 10 years ago.**
- The average person hits what the survey calls "bot fatigue" — the point where interactions feel synthetic — in **40 minutes.**

The methodology is solid enough: 1,200 US adults surveyed in April 2026. It's not a tiny sample. And the findings are consistent with what you see in non-survey data too — DuckDuckGo [saw a 30% increase in users](posts/2026-05-27-google-search-ai-mode-backlash-duckduckgo-tired-of-ai.md) after Google deployed AI Mode. The [German court ruling against Google's AI Overviews](posts/2026-06-11-germany-munich-court-google-ai-overviews-liable-defamation.md) earlier this month was possible partly because the AI-generated summaries had demonstrably eroded consumer trust. People are noticing, quantifiably.

---

## Why Marketing Teams Don't Care (Yet)

If I had to predict what most enterprise marketing teams are doing with this data, I'd guess: filing it, noting it as interesting, and continuing to optimize for AI search visibility at full speed.

The reason is incentive structures. The metrics that get CMOs paid are traffic, lead generation, and pipeline creation. AI search referrals are currently *growing* traffic — 60% of enterprise teams confirmed this in the same survey. If AI search is sending more visitors, and those visitors convert, the fact that a different cohort is being repelled by AI brand language is a secondary concern.

The problem is that these two effects are not hitting the same population. The traffic driven by AI search engines is low-intent discovery traffic — people who got an AI summary and clicked through to the source, often out of skepticism or curiosity. The people being repelled by "AI" in brand messaging are the same audience that's trying to decide whether to buy something, subscribe to something, or trust a brand. Those are not the same people in the same moment.

You can have growing top-of-funnel traffic from AI while simultaneously destroying brand trust with the audience you actually need to convert. Both are true at once.

## The Specific Damage "AI" Does in Brand Language

Here's the thing about the word "AI" in brand messaging: it used to be a differentiator. In 2021, calling your product "AI-powered" conveyed sophistication and modernity. In 2023, it started sounding like marketing copy. In 2025, it sounded like spin. In 2026, per this data, it sounds like a warning label.

Why? Because AI brand claims are nearly always unverifiable by the consumer, they routinely fail to deliver the implied promise, and they are so ubiquitously applied (to everything from thermostats to toothbrushes) that the word carries zero signal and maximum cynicism.

When a brand says "our AI writes personalized emails for you," the consumer's experience is: I get spam that sounds slightly less obviously templated than before. When a brand says "AI-powered recommendations," the consumer's experience is: I see suggestions that are weird and frequently wrong. The gap between the claim and the lived experience has created a Pavlovian response — "AI" in marketing copy now primes skepticism rather than interest.

The ServiceNow executive quoted in the WP VIP report put it well: "No customer or user wakes up and says, 'I hope I get to talk to a chat bot or an AI agent today.' Human-centered design is truer today with artificial intelligence. Ironically, the answer is using AI to be more human."

That's exactly right, and it explains the paradox. The correct move is to use AI extensively in your product without leading with the word "AI" in your customer-facing communication. Do the thing. Don't announce the ingredient.

## Who Has Actually Figured This Out

The brands that consistently perform well in trust metrics in 2026 tend to share a pattern: they don't brand their AI separately. They just make their product better and describe what it does, not how it works.

Look at Notion. Notion has extensive AI features — it rewrites, summarizes, autocompletes, generates entire documents. The interface says "improve writing" and "summarize" and "continue writing." Not "NOTION AI IS TRANSFORMING YOUR DOCUMENT." The AI is ambient. The outcome is what gets described.

Contrast this with virtually every SaaS product that launched an "AI tier" in 2023–2024. The pattern: existing product + AI feature labeled as separate product + 30% price increase + badge on every page that says "AI-Powered." The customer sees: we added a feature and raised prices, and we want credit for it.

The gap between "here's what this does for you" and "here's the technology powering it" is where trust lives.

## The Double Bind for Enterprise Content Teams

The WP VIP survey identifies a genuinely difficult problem for enterprise content teams that the coverage has mostly glossed over.

You now have two audiences with opposite needs:

**AI crawlers and search engines** need structured, machine-readable content. They want clear entity marking, consistent formatting, attributable claims, and semantic clarity. Optimizing for AI discoverability means structuring your content so that a language model can summarize it accurately and cite it credibly. This is the new SEO.

**Human visitors** — the ones who actually click through and buy things — want content that feels human. They're arriving from an AI summary already primed with some version of your content. They want to trust you. They want to sense judgment, voice, and authenticity. The flat, AI-optimized content that machines love often lands as robotic and untrustworthy to the human who arrives after the machine pointed them there.

You cannot fully optimize for both simultaneously. The brands that figure out this navigation first will have a significant advantage — not in AI discoverability, but in the conversion rate after AI delivers the traffic. Right now, nobody has solved it. The WP VIP data confirms this: 61% of consumers couldn't name a brand that was doing AI messaging well. The category has no winner.

---

## What the Smart Move Actually Looks Like

I'll close with what I think the actionable version of this insight is, because "consumers are skeptical of AI" isn't useful without an application.

**Stop leading with AI in any customer-facing context.** Describe what the product does. If your AI-powered search is faster and more accurate, say "search results in under 50ms, surfacing relevant results you didn't think to query." Not "our AI-powered search understands your intent."

**Use "AI" internally and in technical contexts.** Developer documentation, API specs, B2B procurement conversations — these audiences actually want to know what technology is under the hood. Consumer marketing audiences don't.

**Make the AI invisible by making the outcome remarkable.** If your product uses AI to do something that genuinely delights a user, they'll notice the result and form trust. If the AI occasionally fails while being prominently labeled, they'll remember the failure and associate it with the label.

**Earn trust on the way in, not with the headline.** The 33% of consumers who cite "clicking through to the original source" as their top trust signal are telling you something specific: they want to verify. Build landing pages that reward verification. Give them the methodology. Show the source. Let them confirm your AI summary was accurate.

The brands that win the next phase of AI adoption won't be the ones who successfully told consumers "we're AI-powered." They'll be the ones whose consumers never thought about it, because the product just worked.
