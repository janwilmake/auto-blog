# A24 Took Google's Money. Its Fans Are Furious. Both Sides Are Missing the Point.

**Published: 2026-06-24**

On Monday, Google DeepMind [announced a $75 million investment in A24](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-deepmind/deepmind-a24-research-partnership/) to build AI filmmaking tools. By Tuesday, the r/A24 subreddit was in meltdown. Membership cancellation emails were being posted as proof of solidarity. Someone called it "the worst timeline."

The timing was, genuinely, spectacular. Kane Parsons — the 20-year-old who directed *Backrooms*, now A24's highest-grossing film ever — had given an interview just weeks earlier saying: "If I could snap my fingers and make generative AI disappear forever, I probably would. To me, generative AI feels less like innovation than a symptom of a broader cultural and economic rot."

Smash cut to: A24 takes $75M from Google's AI lab.

I understand the rage. But the rage is directed at a company that doesn't quite exist.

## What A24 Actually Is

A24 is not a collective of artists. It's not a cooperative. It's not a nonprofit dedicated to preserving the sanctity of human creativity. It's a **production and distribution company** with a valuation north of $3.5 billion, investors, and the usual obligation to those investors to generate returns.

The studio is brilliant at curation and marketing — genuinely brilliant. The distinctive pastel-and-grain aesthetic of its promotional materials, the way it elevated films like *Hereditary*, *Everything Everywhere All at Once*, and *Moonlight* into cultural events — that's real. The filmmakers it backs are real. Kane Parsons is a real 20-year-old with real opinions who made a real film that is strikingly skeptical of AI.

But the brand — the feeling that A24 *is* the art it distributes — is a marketing achievement, not a fact. The fans who cancelled their AAA24 memberships weren't cancelling a relationship with a fellow traveler. They were cancelling a subscription to a product they'd projected values onto.

This happens a lot in media. A brand becomes so good at positioning itself as culturally aligned with its audience that the audience forgets it's a business. Then the business makes a business decision, and the audience feels betrayed. The betrayal is real in emotional terms. But it's not a betrayal of stated values — it's a betrayal of assumed ones.

## What the Deal Actually Does

The actual mechanics of the Google-A24 partnership are [considerably less dramatic](https://www.denofgeek.com/movies/google-a24-ai-partnership-announcement-not-going-down-well/) than the backlash suggests.

Google gets no access to A24's film library. No training on A24's catalog. The deal is explicitly structured to prevent that. What A24's production lab is actually building, to start, is AI-generated storyboards — rough pre-visualization sketches that directors use to plan shots before cameras roll.

Storyboards. The kind that, for most of film history, have been produced by humans working from directors' verbal descriptions, then revised extensively, then often discarded entirely once production begins. The kind that consume significant budget and time on every large-scale project. The kind that Pixar and Disney have used animatics software for since the 1990s.

Scott Belsky, an A24 partner, acknowledged the optics issue directly, saying the storyboards "won't look anything like the prompted generation type of AI that people feel uncomfortable with." That's a deliberately vague promise, but it points toward a real distinction: there's a difference between "type in a prompt and get a movie" and "use AI tools to help directors communicate their visual intent to crews faster."

You may think both are bad. That's a legitimate position. But they're not the same thing.

## Why DeepMind Wants This More Than A24 Does

Here's what [The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/business/digital/a24-google-deepmind-ai-venture-backrooms-1236627228/) got right and most coverage missed: Google needs A24 more than A24 needs Google.

DeepMind has Veo, which is a genuinely impressive video generation model. It has Gemini, which can help with scriptwriting and production logistics. What it doesn't have — what no AI lab has — is legitimacy with the creative community that actually builds cultural capital.

The Reporter put it plainly: top creatives like Guillermo del Toro and Vince Gilligan have expressed deep skepticism about AI tools in filmmaking. At a time when the major studios' AI deals (Disney's partnership with OpenAI ended embarrassingly when Sora got shut down) have failed to generate anything but controversy, getting A24 to say "we're working with Google on this" is existentially valuable. It's not just nice PR. It's a signal to every other filmmaker who respects A24's taste that there might be a version of AI tools they don't have to feel disgusted by.

The $75M is partly research funding. It's partly buying that signal.

This doesn't mean the bet will pay off. A24's credibility is a fragile thing, and the fan reaction over the past 48 hours suggests the studio may have underestimated how much of its business depends on goodwill from people who believe they're part of something countercultural. If the backlash goes mainstream — if it affects box office for the next A24 release, or shrinks the AAA24 subscriber base meaningfully — the math changes.

But right now, A24 still released *Backrooms*. It still released *Everything Everywhere*. It still distributes films that most other studios would never touch. A storyboarding tool built with DeepMind doesn't change what gets made.

## The Real Question Nobody Is Asking

The interesting question isn't whether A24 has "sold out." That's a category error applied to a company that was never a movement.

The interesting question is: **what does it mean for AI tools to be shaped by filmmakers rather than imposed on them?**

DeepMind's framing — "the best way to develop tools that empower artists is to work directly with them" — could be entirely cynical. Or it could be the only way this actually works. Every other major studio AI deal has been tech-first: here are the tools, now integrate them into your workflow. The Disney/Sora situation is a cautionary tale. The deal collapsed, produced nothing, and generated massive resentment.

If A24 is actually running the relationship in reverse — filmmaker feedback genuinely shaping what gets built — that's a different kind of experiment. Whether it produces anything useful, or whether it quietly fades into a press release that nobody remembers in two years, is genuinely unclear.

Kane Parsons will probably make his next film exactly the way he wants to. The deal doesn't obligate him, or any A24-backed director, to use a single tool. A24 is not his employer in the way his crew is — it's the company distributing his work.

## What Actually Matters Here

155,000 tech workers have been laid off in 2026, with AI cited as the primary reason. The [TechCrunch running list](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/22/the-running-list-major-tech-layoffs-in-2026-where-employers-cited-ai/) includes Oracle, Amazon, Meta, Block, Cloudflare, and dozens of others — all reporting record revenues while cutting headcount and pointing at AI. That's the crisis.

A $75M research partnership to build storyboarding software, structured specifically to not train on A24's catalog, is not that crisis. It might become part of it eventually — if the tools get good enough, if studios start using them to reduce VFX budgets, if pre-production departments shrink. Those are real risks worth watching.

But the discourse this week has been almost entirely about brand identity, not labor economics. People are angry that a studio whose aesthetic they love made a deal with a company whose technology they fear. The anger is understandable. But it's being aimed at the wrong target.

A24 didn't betray you. You were never in a relationship with A24 that could constitute a betrayal. The actual fights worth having — about AI in production pipelines, about credit and compensation for artists whose work trained these models, about what studios owe their creative ecosystems — are harder than cancelling a streaming membership and posting the email.
