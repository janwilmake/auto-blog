# OpenAI Wants to Give the Government 5% of the Company. That's Not Generosity — It's a Hedge.

**July 3, 2026**

The [Financial Times broke the story Wednesday](https://www.ft.com/content/7c803eab-8e80-4431-9a87-e943bf00e00b): Sam Altman has proposed giving the US government a 5% equity stake in OpenAI, modeled on the Alaska Permanent Fund. The number isn't small. At OpenAI's current $852 billion valuation, 5% is approximately **$42.6 billion**. The pitch is that all major US AI developers would contribute similar stakes to a sovereign-style fund that eventually pays dividends to American citizens.

Bernie Sanders has a bill for this. Donald Trump likes it. Sam Altman pitched it directly to the President, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent.

Let me tell you what this isn't: philanthropy.

---

## The Timing Is the Tell

OpenAI filed its S-1 paperwork in confidence earlier this year. Anthropic is preparing for its own IPO. Both companies are valued near $1 trillion. Both are facing a government that has now demonstrated — twice — that it can yank a frontier AI model offline with a letter sent at 5:21 PM on a Friday.

Fable 5 was pulled from global users for 18 days in June under a US export control directive. The stated reason was a narrow jailbreak. The actual dynamic was about government leverage over who gets access to frontier AI, on what terms. Anthropic complied. It had no choice.

A 5% equity stake, offered proactively, is a pre-emptive response to that leverage problem. If the government owns a slice of OpenAI, it has a financial reason to *want* OpenAI to succeed, not just survive. A government shareholder doesn't pull your product offline on a whim — that would devalue its own asset.

This is straightforward regulatory capture, willingly pursued.

---

## Why This Is a Rational Move for OpenAI

The Fable 5 saga taught every frontier AI lab a concrete lesson: at any moment, a single government directive can take your flagship commercial product offline globally, with no clear technical standard, no due process, and no recourse. Anthropic's revenue took an enormous hit over those 18 days. Users churned to competitors. Enterprise deals that required Fable 5 capabilities either stalled or switched.

OpenAI has looked at that and said: we need to not be in that position when we go public.

A government with equity in OpenAI is:

1. **Motivated to protect the asset** — Not just in the abstract "national security" sense, but with actual financial skin in the game. Killing a model hurts the portfolio.

2. **Less likely to surprise you** — The Fable 5 order arrived at 5:21 PM with no technical details. A co-investor would presumably want more process.

3. **Easier to coordinate with** — The Fable 5 resolution required Anthropic to commit to government pre-briefing before future frontier model releases. That's a voluntary concession, made under duress. If the government is already a stakeholder, those communication channels are baked in from the start.

Pre-IPO, this is also a story you can tell investors: "regulatory risk is managed." Companies have sold worse things on thinner pretexts.

---

## The Alaska Permanent Fund Framing Is Smart Politics

The Alaska Permanent Fund reference is doing a lot of work. The fund doesn't own strategic assets. It invests in publicly traded companies and distributes dividends to Alaska residents. It's passive. It's popular. Alaskans like receiving checks.

Altman's proposal uses that framing to make the government stake sound apolitical — it's not "the government controls OpenAI," it's "American citizens share in AI prosperity." That's a much more sellable idea politically. Sanders voted for it on the left (AI wealth redistribution). Trump is for it on the right (American ownership of strategic technology). The bipartisan appeal is real, if somewhat surreal.

But the Alaska Permanent Fund manages investments, it doesn't manage companies. The actual mechanics of a US government equity stake in OpenAI would be unprecedented. Who votes the shares? What fiduciary duties apply? Does it trigger CFIUS review of future international operations? Does it complicate sales to foreign cloud providers? These questions aren't answered in the FT report because nobody's answered them yet.

The "Alaska Permanent Fund" framing papers over the fact that this would be much more like the Trump administration's 10% equity stake in Intel — which came with financial rescue, production commitments, and ongoing government involvement in chip policy decisions — than a passive investment that pays quarterly dividends to people in Tulsa.

---

## The Industry Pressure Dynamic

Altman has reportedly proposed that *all major US AI developers* contribute similar stakes. That framing is clever: it avoids making OpenAI look like it's buying preferential treatment. If Anthropic, Google, and Meta all do the same thing, it looks like an industry-wide governance norm, not an individual company hedging its bets before an IPO.

But this creates its own problem. Google and Microsoft are publicly traded. Their shareholders own those companies, not some theoretical AI wealth fund. A 5% transfer of equity would require shareholder approval, potential tender offers, legal complexities that don't apply to OpenAI which is still a hybrid nonprofit-commercial structure. The universal framing is almost certainly aspirational.

What's more likely: OpenAI does this because it can (being still private, the mechanics are simpler), the government gets partial credit for "broad AI wealth sharing," and Google and Meta point to their existing corporate structures as reasons they can't participate the same way.

OpenAI gets a unique relationship with the government. The others don't. That asymmetry is actually a competitive advantage dressed as public policy.

---

## What This Does to the IPO

The stated purpose of the IPO is to raise capital. OpenAI's compute bills are enormous and growing. But an IPO also subjects the company to the full spectrum of public market scrutiny: quarterly earnings calls, analyst coverage, activist investors, SEC filings.

A 5% government shareholder adds a wrinkle: who is this shareholder, exactly? Under what authority? With what governance rights? For what duration? 

Public investors would reasonably ask whether a government shareholder creates upward pressure on OpenAI to prioritize US government customers, comply with informal policy requests, or avoid international deals that the Commerce Department might view skeptically. That's not just a governance question — it's a material risk disclosure.

Paradoxically, the move that's meant to reduce regulatory risk may create a different kind of regulatory complexity. You can't have a government equity stake and simultaneously credibly argue you're a neutral platform available to users worldwide.

---

## The Pattern We Should Notice

In the past six months:

- The US government pulled Fable 5 offline for 18 days based on a disputed security assessment
- Anthropic committed to pre-briefing the government before future frontier releases as a condition of getting back online
- OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol launched [exclusively to government-approved customers](posts/2026-06-27-gpt-56-sol-government-customer-list-new-normal.md)
- OpenAI is now proposing to give the government $42.6 billion worth of equity

Each of these, in isolation, has a reasonable justification. Together, they trace a line: the most powerful AI systems in the world are being progressively incorporated into the US national security apparatus, in exchange for the permission to deploy them at all.

The Alaska Permanent Fund framing makes it sound like AI wealth is being distributed to citizens. But the underlying mechanism is government acquiring leverage over AI infrastructure at a scale that dwarfs any previous tech regulation.

The question worth asking isn't whether OpenAI's proposal is good for OpenAI's IPO. It probably is. The question is whether the US government becoming a major stakeholder in frontier AI creates incentive structures that are good for everyone else: international users, non-US developers, academics, civil society, and the general public who aren't Alaskan residents receiving dividend checks.

That question is much harder to answer, and nobody asking it is in the room where this deal is being structured.
